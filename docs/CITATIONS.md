# Citations for every claim DischargeIQ makes about patients

Canonical source list for the statistics used in the pitch deck, the NABC
presentation, the clinical brief and the final summary report. Cite from here
rather than restating a number from memory.

Written in response to item 2.4 of the Dr. Liebovitz faculty review (22 Aug
2026): *"Source the headline statistic. The 40 to 80% figure for forgotten or
misunderstood instructions traces to Kessels (2003) and related work; cite it
directly. Tie the readmission claim to a specific measurable outcome rather
than to the general association."*

An unsourced headline number is the first thing a reviewer checks. Every
number this project puts in front of a clinician, a funder or a patient
belongs in this file with a reference.

## The headline statistic

> **40 to 80% of the medical information patients receive is forgotten
> immediately.**

**Kessels RPC. "Patients' memory for medical information." *Journal of the
Royal Society of Medicine.* 2003;96(5):219-222.**
https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/014107680309600504

This is the correct attribution for the figure. Two further findings in the
same paper are worth using, because they strengthen the case rather than
merely establishing it:

- **Nearly half of the information patients do remember is remembered
  incorrectly.** This matters more to DischargeIQ than the forgetting figure.
  A patient who misremembers a warning sign is not helped by being handed the
  same document again; they need it in language they can hold on to.
- **Written summaries improve recall by 20.8%.** This is direct evidence for
  the product's premise, from the same source as the problem statement.

Kessels also identifies the causes of forgetting: difficult medical
terminology, the mode of delivery, and patient factors such as low education.
The first is precisely what the six-agent pipeline targets.
